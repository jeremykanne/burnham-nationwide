The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law this summer with rare bipartisan support. It's the biggest federal housing bill in decades. But if you spend your days permitting and project delivery, the bill isn't what changes your week. What matters is figuring out when a law like this actually reaches your project. 

The honest answer: not at the bill's passing, and not from Washington. A federal law can't rezone your parcel or shorten your plan review. That still happens closer to home, city by city, department by department. What a federal law does is set a direction and give local governments a reason to move. Watching for that movement, and knowing what it means before it lands on a permit, is what counts.

We've seen this pattern before. The ADA wasn't a building code. It was a federal expectation that states turned into enforceable detail. Illinois wrote its own accessibility code that inspectors and plan reviewers could actually apply. The federal law opened the conversation. The local codes did the work. Housing affordability is following the same path, and it's already showing up in the details our clients deal with every day.

Take single-stair reform.

The Act's point-access block pilot puts federal weight behind single-staircase apartment buildings, the layouts common in Europe that free up floor space and make small lots work. But the Act doesn't let you build one. It allows HUD to award competitive grants for pilot programs to assess the feasibility of point-access block buildings. The federal government is nudging. The local code is what eventually turns a nudge into a permit.

Or take pre-approved plans.

The Act also authorizes a HUD grant program to help communities establish pre-approved housing designs or pattern books to speed up local construction processes. Countries like Canada and Australia, U.S. cities like South Bend, IN, and states like California and Hawaii have already implemented programs like this. Chicago, which landed dead last among the ten major metros in residential unit permits issued in 2024 according to one industry analytics service, may see this as an opportunity to turn that number around.

None of this made national headlines, but it moves faster than a bill and it matters more to anyone trying to get a project approved. That's where Burnham stays focused. A ceremony in Washington won't change your zoning approval or your timeline. The local amendment will, quietly, weeks before most people notice. We track those changes across every market we serve, so our clients have the context they need before it affects their project.
