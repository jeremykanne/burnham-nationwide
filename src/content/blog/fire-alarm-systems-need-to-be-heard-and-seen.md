As we all know, emergency preparedness is paramount in today's world. In the not-too-distant past, individuals who are hearing impaired did not have adequate access to emergency alerts. Available visual emergency alerting systems currently in use provide equal access for safely evacuating during emergencies.

 Where a fire alarm system is required, visual alarms are mandated for public and common areas in commercial and residential spaces but had not specifically been in employee work areas or referenced for all dwelling units.

 For employee work areas with audible coverage, the 2010 Americans with Disabilities Act Standards for Accessible Design, was the first to address this by requiring a wiring system for future installation of visual alarms in all employee work areas which would include private offices, whether in a business use setting, but also in uses such as mercantile, residential and assembly. <em>(</em>[*https://www.access-board.gov/ada/#ada-215\_3*](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/#ada-215_3)<em>)</em>

 The 2018 Illinois Accessibility Code elevated compliance requirements per 215.3 to include visible alarms at time of initial construction which also includes areas being altered. It is our understanding that only the areas being altered are required to provide visible alarms unless the entire fire alarm system is being upgraded within a tenant space.

 <em>(See sections 215.2 & 215.3 of</em> [*https://www2.illinois.gov/cdb/announcements/2018/Documents/2018%20Illinois%20Accessibility%20Code.pdf*](https://www2.illinois.gov/cdb/announcements/2018/Documents/2018%20Illinois%20Accessibility%20Code.pdf)<em>)</em>

 The 2019 Chicago Building Code (CBC) has also incorporated similar language which we understand is currently being enforced during reviews of fire alarm drawings and during field inspections.

<em>(</em>[*https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4\_Ch09\_Sec907.5.2.3.1*](https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4_Ch09_Sec907.5.2.3.1)<em>)</em>

 In addition to the common, public and employee areas, there are requirements per CBC (as adopted per the 2018 International Building Code) that require the future ability to provide visible smoke alarms or other visible appliances in residential dwelling units.

 <em>(See section 907.5.2.3.1 of</em> [*https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4\_Ch09\_Sec907.5.2.3.1*](https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4_Ch09_Sec907.5.2.3.1)<em>)</em>

The CBC further specifically indicates that visible alarms shall be arranged so that the flashing light can be seen from all occupiable spaces within the unit and installed as requested by an occupant with a disability. Spaces would include the kitchen, living room, bedroom(s), and bathroom(s). Where possible, a single conduit could be located so devices could be back-to-back (living room to bedroom).

 <em>(</em>[*https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4\_Ch09\_Sec907.5.2.3.3*](https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/CHIBC2019P4/chapter-9-fire-protection-and-life-safety-systems#CHIBC2019P4_Ch09_Sec907.5.2.3.3)<em>)</em>

 Further, per NFPA 72, when installing wall visual alarms or plates for these, location and placement requires that the entire lens be more than 80” and less than 96” above finished floor unless as specified using a performance-based alternative. Often these are at 80” from the floor to the centerline of the device, not bottom of the lens.

It is important for the team to verify that all applicable first tenant build out or interior alterations in Illinois are being provided with the required visible fire alarm devices. In addition check that accommodations are being made for future installation in dwelling units as per your local municipal code.

Are visible (strobe) fire alarms required in every room of an apartment, or just common areas? Under the Chicago Building Code, common and public areas require visible alarms outright. For individual dwelling units, the code requires the future capability to install visible alarms - wiring and infrastructure must be in place - so units can be seen from every occupiable space (kitchen, living room, bedrooms, bathrooms) if a tenant needs one, even if a strobe isn't installed from day one. What's the difference between the ADA's requirement and Illinois' requirement for visible alarms in workplaces? The 2010 ADA Standards only required wiring for future visible alarm installation in employee work areas with audible coverage. The 2018 Illinois Accessibility Code went further, requiring visible alarms to actually be installed at the time of initial construction (and in altered areas), not just wired for future use. If I'm only renovating part of a tenant space, do I need to add visible alarms throughout the whole space? Generally no - only the specific areas being altered need visible alarms added, unless the renovation includes upgrading the entire fire alarm system within that tenant space, in which case broader compliance may be triggered. How high should a visible alarm device be mounted? Per NFPA 72, the entire lens must be positioned more than 80 inches and less than 96 inches above the finished floor, unless a performance-based alternative is used. In practice, this is often achieved by centering the device at 80 inches rather than measuring from the bottom of the lens. Can one strobe device serve two adjacent rooms, like a bedroom and living room? Where layout allows, yes - a single conduit can sometimes be positioned so devices are installed back-to-back between adjoining spaces, which can simplify wiring without sacrificing visibility coverage in either room.
