## Why Multi-Jurisdiction Permitting Creates Compounding Schedule Risk

A single permit delay on a standalone project is manageable. When that same delay pattern repeats across a portfolio of concurrent projects in different cities, the aggregate impact on your program schedule is not additive. It is multiplicative.

Each jurisdiction introduces its own variables: different code editions, different plan review sequences, different departmental structures, and different informal expectations about how applications should be assembled.

For a general contractor opening locations in three states, the permitting process is not one workflow repeated three times. It is three distinct regulatory environments, each with its own queue dynamics and resubmission cycles.

The practical consequence is that your construction crews, lease commencement dates, and capital deployment timelines all become hostage to the slowest jurisdiction in your portfolio.

According to a [2026 NAHB special study](https://www.nahb.org/news-and-economics/housing-economics-plus/special-studies/special-studies-pages/government-regulation-in-the-price-of-new-homes-2026), regulations imposed by government at all levels now account for 26.4% of the final price of a new single-family home built for sale.

While that figure covers all regulatory costs, the permitting component is where multi-jurisdiction projects face the steepest friction. Those costs compound across every location in a portfolio.

## How to Quantify the Cost of Permit Delays on Multi-Site Projects

Building a business case for national permit expediting requires converting schedule risk into dollar figures your finance team and ownership group can evaluate. The costs of permit delays fall into three categories: direct carrying costs, rework expenses, and opportunity costs.

### Direct Carrying Costs During Permit Delays

Every week a project sits waiting for permit approval, your organization is paying for it. Lease obligations continue. Insurance premiums accrue. Construction crews may be held on standby or redeployed at a premium.

Equipment rental agreements do not pause because a building department has a backlog. For a multi-site program, multiply those weekly costs across every delayed location to see the aggregate financial exposure.

The carrying cost calculation is straightforward: sum your weekly lease, insurance, financing, and standby labor costs for each location, then multiply by the expected delay duration. For projects in jurisdictions with documented backlogs, plan on four to eight additional weeks beyond the published review timeline.

### Rework Expenses from Incomplete Submissions

When a permit application is returned for corrections, the cost extends beyond the resubmission itself. Your design team must revisit the drawings, and your engineer of record must address technical comments.

The application then re-enters the review queue behind everything filed in the interim. Each correction cycle typically adds two to six weeks to the timeline, depending on the jurisdiction and the complexity of the comments.

Burnham Nationwide's approach to [early due diligence](/final-review-blog/due-diligence/) is designed to catch submission gaps before they reach the building department. By researching jurisdiction-specific requirements and confirming documentation standards in advance, the goal is to reduce first-round correction rates and avoid the cascading delays that follow a returned application.

### Opportunity Cost of Fragmented Local Relationships

Managing permitting through a patchwork of local expediters creates a coordination overhead that is rarely captured in project budgets. Each local relationship requires its own onboarding, its own communication cadence, and its own quality assurance process. There is no centralized status view, no standardized reporting, and no single point of accountability when something goes wrong.

The opportunity cost is the management time your internal team spends coordinating across those fragmented relationships instead of focusing on construction execution, site selection, or design optimization. For a program manager overseeing twenty concurrent locations, that coordination burden can consume a significant portion of their available bandwidth.

## What to Include in a Business Case for National Permit Expediting

A persuasive business case needs to do more than list the benefits of centralized permitting. It needs to present a clear comparison between the current cost of managing permits in-house or through fragmented local relationships and the projected cost of engaging a national partner. The following framework addresses the key components.

### Baseline Your Current Permitting Costs

Start by documenting what you are spending today. Pull data from your last twelve months of projects: total permit fees paid, expediter fees, internal staff time allocated to permitting, and the number of correction cycles per project.

Calculate the average time from initial submission to permit issuance for each jurisdiction you have worked in. This baseline gives you the denominator for your ROI calculation.

If your organization does not track permitting costs as a discrete line item, that gap is itself a finding worth including in the business case. Projects that cannot measure their [code compliance](/what-we-do/code-consulting/) costs cannot manage them.

### Model the Delay Scenarios

Use your historical data to build three scenarios: a best case (permits issued on first review), a likely case (one correction cycle per jurisdiction), and a worst case (two or more correction cycles in high-complexity jurisdictions). Assign carrying costs to each scenario and calculate the aggregate financial impact across your full portfolio.

For jurisdictions where you lack historical data, use published review timelines as a starting point and add a buffer based on the jurisdiction's documented backlog status. Building departments in high-growth metropolitan areas routinely run four to eight weeks behind their published timelines.

### Compare the National Partner Model Against the Status Quo

Present the comparison in a format your finance team expects: a side-by-side of annual costs under the current approach versus the projected costs with a national partner. Include both the direct fees and the indirect savings from reduced correction cycles and freed staff capacity.

Be conservative in your savings estimates. Even a modest reduction in average permit timelines across a multi-site program produces meaningful savings when multiplied across the full portfolio.

## How National Permit Expediting Reduces Schedule Risk

Schedule risk in multi-jurisdiction projects comes from two sources: the variability between jurisdictions and the unpredictability within any single jurisdiction. A national permit expediting partner addresses both.

### Jurisdiction-Specific Knowledge Reduces First-Round Rejections

Every building department has its own set of informal expectations about how applications should be presented. A submission that clears intake in one city may be returned in another because the file naming convention does not match the portal requirements.

These are not code issues. They are administrative issues, and they are the primary source of avoidable delay in multi-jurisdiction programs.

Burnham Nationwide's team works across [3,000 cities](/nationwide-permitting-process/) nationwide, building institutional knowledge of each jurisdiction's specific requirements. That accumulated experience translates directly into cleaner first-round submissions and fewer correction cycles.

### Centralized Tracking Provides Early Warning on At-Risk Locations

When permitting is managed as a coordinated program rather than a collection of independent projects, your team gains visibility into which locations are progressing on schedule and which are at risk of slipping.

This early warning capability allows you to reallocate resources, adjust construction sequencing, or escalate with the building department before a delay becomes a schedule failure.

Without centralized tracking, at-risk locations often are not identified until the delay has already materialized. By that point, the options for recovery are limited and expensive.

### Single-Point Accountability Eliminates Coordination Gaps

In a fragmented permitting model, accountability is distributed across multiple local expediters, each with their own communication standards and reporting cadences.

When a submission is delayed, identifying the root cause requires your team to investigate across multiple vendors. With a [national partner](/who-we-help/), there is one organization accountable for the entire portfolio and one escalation path when issues arise.

## How to Evaluate National Versus Local Permit Expediter Models

The decision between a national partner and a network of local expediters is not primarily about cost per permit. It is about total program cost, which includes the coordination overhead, the risk of inconsistent submission quality, and the management burden of maintaining multiple vendor relationships.

### When Local Expediters Are Sufficient

If your organization operates in one or two jurisdictions with a small number of concurrent projects, a trusted local expediter may be the most efficient option. The relationship is direct, the communication is simple, and the local knowledge runs deep.

This model works when the coordination overhead is low enough that your internal team can manage it without dedicated resources.

### When a National Partner Becomes Necessary

The inflection point typically arrives when your project count crosses into double digits across three or more jurisdictions simultaneously. At that scale, the coordination overhead of managing individual local relationships exceeds the capacity of most internal permitting teams.

The risk of inconsistent submission quality increases, and the absence of centralized tracking makes it difficult to manage aggregate [schedule risk](/final-review-blog/a-guide-to-managing-permit-timeline-risk-in-2026/).

Other signals that a national partner model is warranted include: repeated first-round rejections in new jurisdictions, internal staff spending more time coordinating vendors than managing construction, and an inability to provide ownership with a reliable portfolio-wide permit status report.

## Common Objections to National Permit Expediting and How to Address Them

"We already have relationships with local expediters who know our jurisdictions." This is a valid starting point, and those relationships have value. The question is whether they scale.

If your expansion plan takes you into five new markets next year, each new jurisdiction requires a new local relationship with its own learning curve and its own risk of first-round rejection.

"The cost of a national partner is higher than our current per-permit spend." Compare total program cost, not per-permit cost. Include the internal coordination time, the cost of correction cycles, and the carrying costs of delayed locations.

For most multi-site programs, the all-in cost of a national partner is lower than the aggregate cost of fragmented local management.

"Our internal team can handle it." At current volume, they may be right. The business case should model what happens at projected volume. If your expansion plan doubles the number of concurrent jurisdictions, can your internal team absorb that increase without adding headcount or accepting longer timelines?

## Step-by-Step: Building Your Business Case Document

The following steps provide a practical framework for assembling a business case that will stand up to scrutiny from your finance team, executive leadership, and project stakeholders.

### Step 1: Audit Your Current Permitting Performance

Gather data from completed projects: average time from submission to permit issuance, number of correction cycles per project, total internal hours allocated to permitting coordination, and total fees paid to local expediters.

If this data does not exist in a centralized format, the audit itself will reveal the operational gaps that a national partner would address.

### Step 2: Quantify the Financial Impact of Delays

For each project in your recent history that experienced a permit delay, calculate the carrying costs incurred during the delay period. Include lease payments, insurance, financing costs, standby labor, and any penalties triggered by missed milestones. Sum these costs across your portfolio to establish the annual cost of permit-related delays.

### Step 3: Project Your Future Permitting Volume

Map your planned expansion against the jurisdictions you will be entering. For each new jurisdiction, estimate the due diligence requirements, likely review timelines, and the probability of correction cycles based on comparable jurisdictions in your historical data. This projection gives you the denominator for your cost-per-permit comparison.

### Step 4: Request Proposals from National Partners

Provide prospective partners with your project list, jurisdictions, and historical permitting data. Ask for a detailed scope of services, a fee structure, and references from clients with comparable [multi-site programs](/final-review-blog/phased-permitting-strategies/). Evaluate proposals not just on cost but on the depth of jurisdictional knowledge, the staffing model, and the reporting capabilities offered.

### Step 5: Present the Comparison with Conservative Assumptions

Build your business case around conservative savings estimates. If your data suggests a national partner could reduce average permit timelines by four weeks per jurisdiction, model the savings at two weeks.

Your finance team will be more receptive to a business case that under-promises. The actual results will validate the decision when they exceed the projections.
