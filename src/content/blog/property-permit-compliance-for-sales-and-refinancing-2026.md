## What Is Property Permit Compliance?

Property permit compliance means that every construction, renovation, or alteration on a building has the required municipal permits, that those permits received final inspection approval, and that the work meets applicable building codes. A property is compliant when its physical condition matches its official permit record.

When a gap exists between the two, the property carries what regulators and lenders consider an unresolved compliance issue. That gap could be a finished basement with no permit on file, an electrical upgrade that was never inspected, or an addition that predates the current owner's tenure.

For commercial properties, the stakes multiply. Multi-tenant buildings, mixed-use developments, and properties with complex mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems often accumulate permit history across decades of ownership changes and renovations.

## Why Do Missing Permits Matter in Real Estate Transactions?

A missing permit does not just represent a paperwork gap. It signals that work was never inspected by a municipal authority, which means no third party has confirmed that the construction meets safety and code standards. That uncertainty affects every party in a transaction.

Buyers face the risk of inheriting code violations they did not create. Sellers face disclosure liability if they knew or should have known about unpermitted work. Lenders face collateral risk if the property's value depends on improvements that cannot be legally recognized.

The practical consequences surface in three areas at once: appraisals, financing, and insurance. An appraiser may reduce the property's value by excluding unpermitted square footage. A lender may refuse to fund or refinance until the issue is resolved. And an insurer may limit or deny coverage for damage connected to an unpermitted area.

## How Open Permits Affect Property Refinancing

An open permit means a municipality authorized work, but the project was never signed off with a final inspection. Open permits transfer to the current property owner at closing and remain on the municipal record indefinitely. They do not expire.

When you apply to refinance a commercial property, the lender evaluates the building as collateral. An open permit raises immediate questions about whether the work was completed properly, whether the property meets code, and whether additional inspections or repairs might be required.

### What Lenders Look for During Refinancing

Lenders assess the property's condition, legal standing, and marketability. If a title search or municipal lien search reveals an open permit, the lender may pause the refinancing process until the issue is addressed.

Depending on the loan program, a lender might require the borrower to close the open permit, provide documentation from a licensed professional, or obtain a letter of no objection from the building department.

According to a [2026 analysis by Minchella & Associates](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/real-estate-and-construction/what-happens-if-there-are-open-permits-on-a-property), open permits frequently surface during routine title reviews and can convert a straightforward closing into weeks of negotiations over documentation and inspection requirements.

### How Open Permits Impact Appraisals

Appraisers evaluate the property based on its condition, comparable sales, and legal use. If unpermitted or incompletely permitted work is identified, the appraiser may not assign full value to the affected areas. A converted warehouse space, a mezzanine addition, or an upgraded HVAC system with no final inspection may be treated differently than legally permitted improvements.

A lower appraisal creates a gap between the refinancing amount you need and the value the lender is willing to recognize. That gap can force you to bring additional capital to the table, renegotiate loan terms, or delay the refinancing until the permit issue is resolved.

## How Permit Issues Disrupt Real Estate Sales

Permit problems affect real estate sales from both the seller's and buyer's perspective. For sellers, undisclosed permit issues can lead to post-sale litigation. For buyers, they create uncertainty about the property's true condition and value.

### Seller Disclosure Obligations

Nearly every state requires sellers to disclose known material defects, including improvements made without required permits. The specifics vary by jurisdiction. Some states use standardized disclosure forms with direct questions about unpermitted work. Others impose a broader duty to reveal any condition that could affect the property's value or safety.

Sellers who fail to disclose known permit issues risk fraud liability. Courts in many jurisdictions hold that sellers cannot escape responsibility by choosing not to investigate obvious irregularities. The financial exposure can include remediation costs, legal fees, and in some cases, punitive damages.

### How Permit Gaps Stall Closings

A permit gap discovered during the closing process can halt a transaction. Buyers may request retroactive permits, price reductions, or credits to cover remediation costs. Lenders may refuse to finance the property until the issue is resolved. Title companies may flag open permits as exceptions on the title commitment.

The timing of discovery matters. Issues identified early in the contract period can often be negotiated. Issues found days before closing create pressure that benefits neither party and can lead to failed transactions.

## Property Permit Compliance in Buyer Due Diligence

Due diligence is where permit compliance issues are most commonly uncovered. A thorough due diligence process goes beyond a standard title search to include a detailed review of the property's permit history, certificate of occupancy status, and code compliance record.

### What a Permit History Review Should Include

A comprehensive permit review involves pulling records from the local building department for every permit associated with the property's address. Look for permits that were issued but never received a final inspection and permits for work different from what was actually built.

Also flag periods where visible renovations have no corresponding permit on file. These gaps are the most common source of compliance issues in commercial transactions.

The key document is the final inspection report. A "closed" or "finaled" permit means a building inspector signed off on every phase of the work and confirmed it met applicable codes. An "open" permit means a permit was pulled and work was started, but the project was never officially completed.

### Why Multi-Jurisdiction Reviews Are Critical

For commercial properties that span multiple locations or fall under overlapping municipal authorities, permit records may be fragmented across different departments and databases. A process that works in one city may trigger rejection in another. Requirements for documentation, submission format, and inspection protocols differ from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.

## Common Types of Permit Compliance Issues

Not all permit issues carry the same level of risk. Understanding the severity helps you prioritize which issues to address before a transaction and which ones may be manageable with proper documentation.

### Unpermitted Construction

This is the most common issue. It includes any construction, renovation, or alteration completed without a required building permit. Examples range from minor cosmetic work to structural additions, electrical rewiring, and plumbing changes that clearly needed municipal approval.

The risk level depends on the scope of the work and whether it affects safety, structural integrity, or legal use of the property.

### Open Permits

An open permit indicates that a permit was issued and work began, but the final inspection was never completed. The original contractor may have finished the job and moved on without scheduling the sign-off. Or the project scope may have changed, leaving the original permit in a limbo that follows the property through every subsequent transaction.

### Expired or Revoked Permits

Some municipalities set time limits on permits. If construction does not begin or reach completion within the allowed window, the permit expires. The owner then needs to apply for a new permit under current codes, which may have changed since the original application. Revoked permits present a more serious concern, as they typically indicate a code violation or failed inspection.

### Certificate of Occupancy Gaps

A certificate of occupancy (CO) confirms that a building is safe for its intended use and complies with applicable codes. If renovations changed the building's use classification, occupancy load, or egress paths without updating the CO, the property may not be legally authorized for its current purpose. This can affect both the sale and the buyer's ability to operate the building as planned.

## How to Verify Permit Records Before a Transaction

Proactive permit verification protects both buyers and sellers from surprises that can derail a deal. The verification process should begin as early as possible and follow a structured approach.

### Step 1: Search the Local Building Department Records

Start with the local building department's online permit portal. Most municipalities maintain searchable databases where you can look up permits by property address. These portals typically show the permit type, issue date, scope of work, and whether the final inspection was completed.

If the online records are incomplete or the jurisdiction has not digitized older files, submit a written records request to the building department. Requests for municipal building records are governed by your state's public records law, not federal FOIA, and the process, fees, and response times vary by jurisdiction.

### Step 2: Compare Records Against the Physical Property

Walk the property and compare what you see against the permit history. Note any visible renovations, additions, or system upgrades that do not have a corresponding permit on file. Pay particular attention to finished basements, mezzanine levels, converted spaces, upgraded electrical panels, and modified fire protection systems.

For commercial properties with complex mechanical and electrical systems, a [code compliance review](/what-we-do/code-consulting/) by a licensed professional can identify discrepancies that a visual walk-through alone might miss.

### Step 3: Engage a Permit Expediter for Resolution

If the review reveals open permits, missing permits, or compliance gaps, the next step is to determine what the municipality requires to resolve each issue. Some jurisdictions allow retroactive or "as-built" permits. Others may require the owner to open walls for inspection, hire licensed contractors, or bring the work up to current code standards.

## Retroactive Permitting: What You Need to Know

Retroactive permitting is the process of obtaining a building permit after the construction or renovation work is already complete. It is available in many municipalities, but the process comes with additional costs, requirements, and risks that you should understand before relying on it as a transaction solution.

### When Retroactive Permits Are Available

Most jurisdictions allow retroactive permitting when the unpermitted work would have been allowed under current building codes. If zoning has changed since the work was completed, or if the construction violates requirements that cannot be corrected without demolition, retroactive permitting may not be an option.

Retroactive permit fees are typically higher than standard permit fees. Some jurisdictions charge double the standard fee as a penalty. Others charge up to five times the original amount. These costs should be factored into any transaction negotiation.

### The Inspection Process for Retroactive Permits

Obtaining a retroactive permit usually requires a licensed professional to inspect the work and confirm it meets current code. In many cases, this means opening walls, ceilings, or floors so inspectors can see the framing, wiring, plumbing, or structural elements behind them.

If the work does not meet code, the owner bears the cost of bringing it into compliance before the permit can be closed. This can range from minor corrections to substantial reconstruction, depending on the scope and quality of the original work.

## Insurance Risks of Incomplete Permit Records

Permit compliance gaps create insurance exposure that many property owners underestimate. The risk extends beyond the immediate transaction to ongoing coverage and claims.

### How Insurers Evaluate Unpermitted Work

Insurance companies assess risk based on the property's condition and compliance status. If damage occurs in or because of an unpermitted area, the insurer may deny the claim. The reasoning is straightforward: work that was never inspected means the insurer never accurately assessed the risk it was covering.

Even when the damage is not directly caused by the unpermitted work, an insurer that discovers code violations during a claim investigation may cancel the policy or refuse renewal.

For commercial property owners, this creates a compounding exposure. You may be simultaneously unable to collect on a claim, unable to obtain new coverage, and unable to complete a sale or refinancing until the insurance issue is addressed.

### What to Discuss with Your Insurance Provider

Before closing any transaction on a property with known permit gaps, verify with the insurance carrier whether the unpermitted work could affect coverage, whether any areas would be excluded from the policy, and whether retroactive permits or inspections would resolve the coverage concern. Address these questions during due diligence, not after closing.

## Property Tax Consequences of Unpermitted Improvements

Unpermitted improvements often escape the tax assessor's records, which means the property's assessed value does not reflect the additional square footage or upgraded features. When the assessor eventually discovers the discrepancy, the owner can face a supplemental tax bill reflecting the increased value.

Some jurisdictions apply this adjustment retroactively, covering the years the improvement went unrecorded. The tax correction lands on the current owner, regardless of who completed the unpermitted work. This risk transfers at closing and should be evaluated as part of the due diligence process.

## A Permit Compliance Checklist for Real Estate Transactions

Use this checklist to organize your permit verification process, whether you are buying, selling, or refinancing a commercial property.

### Before Listing or Applying to Refinance

-   Pull the complete permit history from the local building department.
-   Identify any open permits, expired permits, or periods with no permit activity despite visible renovations.
-   Confirm the certificate of occupancy matches the current use and occupancy classification.
-   Resolve open permits by scheduling final inspections or applying for retroactive permits where allowed.
-   Document all completed work with contractor records, inspection reports, and photographs.

### During Buyer Due Diligence

-   Request permit records and renovation documentation from the seller.
-   Cross-reference the permit history against the physical property condition.
-   Engage a [permit expediter](/about/faq/) or code consultant if discrepancies are found.
-   Notify the lender and insurance carrier early if permit issues are identified.
-   Negotiate resolution terms in the purchase agreement, including who bears the cost of retroactive permits, inspections, and corrections.

### After Closing

-   Store all permit documentation securely with your property records.
-   Monitor for any follow-up inspection requirements from the building department.
-   Establish a permit tracking process for future renovations to maintain compliance going forward.

## FAQs About Property Permit Compliance

### What happens if a property has open permits during a refinance?

An open permit can delay or block a refinance because the lender views unresolved permits as collateral risk. You may need to close the permit by scheduling a final inspection or providing documentation from a licensed professional before the lender approves the loan.

### Can you sell a commercial property with missing building permits?

You can, but you are required to disclose known permit issues in most states. Missing permits may reduce the appraised value, complicate buyer financing, and expose you to post-sale liability if the buyer discovers undisclosed compliance gaps after closing.

### What is a retroactive building permit and when is it available?

A retroactive permit is obtained after construction work is already complete. It is available in many municipalities when the unpermitted work meets current building codes. Fees are typically higher than standard permits, and the process may require inspections that involve opening walls or ceilings.

### How far back should permit records be reviewed during due diligence?

Review the complete permit history for the property, going back as far as records are available. Older commercial buildings may have decades of renovations with incomplete documentation. Cross-reference visible improvements against the permit timeline to identify gaps that need resolution before closing.

### Does title insurance cover losses from unpermitted work?

Standard title insurance policies typically exclude coverage for building code and zoning violations. Enhanced policies may offer limited protection, but they are not a substitute for verifying permit compliance during due diligence. Confirm your specific policy terms with your title company.
